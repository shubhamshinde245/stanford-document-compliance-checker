# Campus Arboretum — Seasonal Tree Care Calendar

**Prepared by:** Grounds and Landscape Services
**Season:** 2026

## About the arboretum

The campus arboretum holds four hundred and twelve catalogued specimens across
eighteen acres, including a coast live oak believed to predate the founding of the
university. This calendar tells the grounds crew and volunteer gardeners what to do in
each part of the year so the collection stays healthy and the older trees keep their
structure.

## Winter — January and February

Winter is the quiet season and the right time for structural pruning of deciduous
specimens. Prune while the branches are bare and the shape of the crown is easy to
read. Remove deadwood, crossing limbs, and any branch rubbing against another. Do not
prune the flowering cherries in winter; wait until they have finished blooming or you
will lose the display.

Check the stakes and ties on young trees planted in the last three seasons. Ties that
have begun to cut into the bark should be loosened or replaced with a wider strap.

Spread mulch to a depth of three inches around the base of specimen trees, keeping a
clear collar of two inches around the trunk itself. Mulch piled against the bark holds
moisture and invites rot.

## Spring — March, April, and May

Watch for aphids on the tulip trees as the new growth comes in. A strong jet of water
is usually enough. Reach for insecticidal soap only when a colony is established, and
never spray in full sun or you will scorch the leaves.

Feed the magnolias with a slow-release balanced fertiliser as the buds swell. Do not
feed the oaks; they do better without it and heavy feeding pushes soft growth that
splits in the first storm.

Spring is planting season. Set new stock so the root flare sits just above the finished
grade. A tree planted too deep will decline slowly for years and no amount of later
care will fix it. Water new plantings deeply twice a week through the first summer.

## Summer — June, July, and August

Summer work is mostly watering and observation. Deep, infrequent watering encourages
roots to go down; light daily sprinkling keeps them shallow and makes a tree fragile in
drought. Twenty gallons per young tree per week, delivered slowly, is the standard.

Walk the collection weekly and look up. Note any limb that has begun to droop, any
sudden thinning of the canopy, and any mushroom or bracket fungus at the base — that
last one is often the first visible sign of root decay and should be reported to the
arborist the same day.

Do not prune in high summer except to remove a hazard. Cuts made in heat invite borers.

## Autumn — September, October, and November

Leaf collection begins in earnest in October. Shredded leaves are composted on site at
the north yard and returned to the beds the following spring; nothing leaves the
grounds.

Autumn is the second planting window and the better one for most natives, since the
roots establish over the wet months without the stress of summer heat. Plant early
enough that there are six weeks of soil warmth left.

Inspect the veteran oaks before the winter storms. Any limb over six inches in diameter
that shows a crack, a cavity, or included bark at the union should be assessed for
cabling or reduction.

## The veteran oak

The coast live oak at the western end of the lawn is the oldest specimen in the
collection. It is not to be pruned, cabled, or root-disturbed without the written
agreement of the consulting arborist. Compaction is its greatest threat: keep vehicles,
staging, and event tenting entirely outside the drip line, and route foot traffic to
the gravel path.
